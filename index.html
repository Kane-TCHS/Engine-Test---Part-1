01 / Engine theory · 10 points

From motor design to flight behavior

Read, explore, and explain. Each T response is worth 2 points. Use complete explanations and correct units.

1. What is inside a motor?

A commercial solid-propellant motor stores fuel and oxidizer in its propellant. Hot gas escapes backward through the nozzle, producing forward thrust. The casing contains the motor components and the nozzle directs the exhaust.

The propellant produces the powered phase. After burnout, the delay material burns with little useful thrust and often produces tracking smoke. The ejection charge then releases gas to activate the recovery system.

This is a data-analysis activity. The cutaway is a conceptual reference. Use only commercial motors as supplied and follow your teacher’s procedures for any separate launch activity.

The letter gives an impulse range. Each successive full letter class doubles the upper limit. The first number is a nominal average thrust in newtons. The final number gives the nominal delay in seconds from burnout to ejection. Actual measurements can differ from the code.

3. Powered flight, coast, recovery

Ignition begins the powered phase. Thrust can rise rapidly to a peak, settle, and fall at burnout. During the delay, a flying rocket coasts under gravity and aerodynamic forces. Upward motion can continue even after useful thrust ends.

Apogee is the highest point in the flight. A suitable delay aims to deploy recovery near apogee. An overly short delay can deploy recovery while the rocket is still rising quickly. An overly long delay can allow a dangerous descent before deployment.

A stationary motor test measures force. It does not directly measure altitude, speed, or apogee.

4. Thrust is force. Impulse includes time.

Peak thrust is the highest force during the propellant burn. Total impulse is the area under the thrust–time curve from ignition to burnout. It describes momentum change due to thrust and has units of N·s. It is not energy in joules or power in watts.

I = ∫ F(t) dt    F̄ = I / (tburnout − tignition)

The average thrust is the height of a rectangle with the same duration and area as the burn curve. Exclude the delay and ejection event from the burn impulse calculation.

5. Compare performance carefully

Two motors can deliver the same impulse with different thrust histories. One may deliver a short, strong push and another a longer, weaker push. Peak thrust alone cannot tell you the total impulse.

Flight height also depends on rocket mass, drag, and other conditions. The letter class by itself does not guarantee an exact altitude. A measured motor need not exactly reach the top of its class range.

02 / Time vs. thrust · 10 points

Investigate your assigned motor

The original measurements are built in: 401 samples per data set, spaced 0.02 s apart. Identify the burn and delay events using evidence. There is no requirement that your measured values exactly equal the reference chart.

Use the data set assigned by your teacher. Each data set keeps its own analysis entries when you switch.

A. Examine and annotate the curve

  1. Use the full view to find the powered burn. Switch to burn view for detail.
  2. Click a point on the graph, or use the sample slider/arrow keys. The readout gives its time and force.
  3. Choose “Use cursor” for ignition, peak, burnout, and ejection. You can also type an event time. Entries snap to the nearest 0.02 s sample.
  4. Use the delay-detail view to inspect small changes after burnout. The ejection signal may be very weak. Record an evidence-based estimate and discuss uncertainty.

Teal: measured force · Shaded area: selected burn impulse · Dashed line: calculated average thrust · Numbered lines: your event selections

The curve is a sampled measurement with noise and drift. The delay-detail view expands its force scale. A small bump is evidence to evaluate, not proof by itself. If an event is unresolved, use a tentative time and explain the limit, or leave it blank and explain why.

B. Turn the curve into measurements

The tool uses the trapezoidal rule on the original samples in your selected interval: I ≈ Σ[(Fᵢ + Fᵢ₊₁)/2] × Δt, with Δt = 0.02 s. Baseline correction applies before integration. Signed samples are retained. No smoothing or automatic event detection is applied.

C. Identify the engine with evidence

Compare your measured impulse, peak force, burn duration, and delay with the supplied Estes chart. The reference impulse values often sit at class limits. The code’s first number is nominal and may not equal your measured average or reference impulse divided by duration.

The historical chart states a delay tolerance of ±10% or ±1 s, whichever is greater. Use several measurements together. Discuss discrepancies instead of forcing an exact match.
